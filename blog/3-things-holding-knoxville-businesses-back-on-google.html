3 Things Holding Most Local Businesses Back on Google

July 2026 · Knoxville, TN

I audit local businesses every week and I keep finding the same three problems over and over. I recorded a short video walking through all three — watch it below, then keep reading for the full breakdown.

• • •

1. Your Google Business Profile is half-empty.

Google gives you up to 99 service slots on your profile. Most businesses I audit have zero filled in. Google asks you dozens of questions — payment methods, accessibility, service highlights. Most are blank. There is a booking link feature that lets customers contact you with one click. Most do not have it set up.

Every empty field is a missed signal. Google is deciding who deserves the top three spots, and it uses everything you give it. When your profile is half-complete, you are telling Google you are half a business.

I found this exact problem in a Powell HVAC company that was already ranking in the top three. They had no booking link, no schema on their website, and their GBP services were not filled out. They were winning despite those gaps — but any competitor who fills theirs in could take that spot.

The fix: go into your Google Business Profile and fill in every single field. Add at least 30 services with descriptions. Set up the booking link. Answer every question Google asks. This alone can move your ranking.

2. Your website is not telling Google what you do or where you do it.

Your website might look great. Professional design, nice photos, clean layout. But Google does not see what you see. Google reads code.

What Google is looking for is very specific. Your title tag — the text that shows up in the browser tab — needs to include your main service and your city. Your H1 heading needs to match. If your title tag just says your business name and nothing else, Google has to guess what you do and where. It will not guess in your favor.

Beyond the title tag, Google is looking for service pages and area pages. If you are a plumber who serves Knoxville, Farragut, Powell, and Maryville, you should have a dedicated page for each area. Each page is a content opportunity — if someone in Powell searches for a plumber and you do not have a page targeting Powell, you cannot compete for that search. The businesses ranking in the top three almost always have significantly more indexed pages than the businesses below them.

When I audited Friendly Neighborhood Plumber here in Knoxville, their title tag and H1 were both perfect — "Plumbing Service in Knoxville, Tennessee." They also had dedicated area pages for every town they serve. That is exactly what Google wants to see. On the other hand, one Oak Ridge roofing company I reviewed did not have a website at all. Businesses from Knoxville — twenty minutes away — were ranking in Oak Ridge instead of them.

The fix: check your title tag. Does it say "[Your Service] in [Your City]"? Is your H1 heading the same? Do you have dedicated pages for the areas you serve? Is your address in the footer as plain text? If the answer to any of these is no, that is directly costing you rankings.

3. Your business information is not linked across the internet.

Google does not just look at your website and your Google Business Profile in isolation. It looks at the entire internet to see if your business information shows up consistently across other trusted websites. These are called citations — and they are how Google builds confidence that your business is real, legitimate, and where you say you are.

Think of it like references on a resume. If your name, address, and phone number are confirmed on Yelp, Facebook, Apple Maps, Nextdoor, BBB, Bing Places, and dozens of other directories, Google creates a web of trust around your business. The more consistent, high-quality citations you have, the stronger that trust signal becomes.

Most businesses I audit have the basics — maybe Yelp, maybe Facebook. But the businesses ranking in the top three have significantly more. When I audited Monster Tree Service of Knoxville, they had a citation on the West Knox Chamber of Commerce. That single local listing carries more weight than a dozen generic national directories because it ties the business directly to the community.

And here is the detail that trips up most business owners: your information has to be identical everywhere. If your Google profile says "2503 W Beaver Creek Dr" and your Yelp listing says "2503 West Beaver Creek Drive," that inconsistency creates doubt. I find NAP mismatches — "Road" versus "Rd," old phone numbers on forgotten listings — in almost every audit I do.

The fix: get your business listed on as many high-quality directories as possible with the exact same name, address, and phone number everywhere. Start with the big ones — Yelp, Facebook, Apple Maps, Bing Places, Nextdoor — then expand into niche-specific and local community directories. And audit your existing listings for inconsistencies. An afternoon of cleanup can remove doubt from every algorithm Google runs on your business.

Knowing the problems is not the same as knowing YOUR problems.

These three fixes will get you moving in the right direction. But here is where self-diagnosis breaks down: you do not know how your GBP compares to the specific competitors above you. You do not know if your website's code is actually structured the way Google needs it. You do not know which citations have old addresses, wrong phone numbers, or missing information. You do not know what you do not know — and that is exactly what keeps businesses stuck at position twelve instead of position three.
