Why Is My Business Not Showing Up on Google?

I audit local businesses in Knoxville every week. Plumbers, tree service companies, HVAC contractors, roofers. I record the whole thing on video so the owner can see exactly what I see.

And almost every single time, the owner is surprised by the same thing: they had no idea they were not showing up on Google.

They assumed they were. They have a Google Business Profile. They have a website. They have been in business for years. Customers know them. So they must be showing up, right?

Then I share my screen, search their main service plus their city, and they are nowhere. Not in the top three. Not on the first page. Sometimes not at all.

The top three results on Google get more than half of every click. If you are not in those three spots, you are paying for invisibility.

If you are a local business owner in Knoxville or anywhere in East Tennessee and you have had that sinking feeling that your phone should be ringing more — here is what is probably going on, based on what I find in real audits every week.

• • •

Your Google Business Profile Is Incomplete

This is the single most common problem I find. Business owners set up their Google Business Profile when they first opened, filled in the basics — name, phone number, hours — and never touched it again.

Google gives you up to 99 service slots. Most businesses I audit have zero filled in. Google asks you dozens of questions about your business — accessibility, payment methods, service highlights, amenities. Most are left blank. Google lets you add a booking link so customers can contact you with one click. Most do not have one.

Every empty field is a missed signal. Google is trying to figure out who deserves those top three spots, and it uses everything you give it. When your profile is half-empty, you are telling Google you are half a business.

I found this exact problem in a Powell HVAC company that was already ranking in the top three. They had no booking link, no schema on their website, and their GBP services were not filled out. They were winning despite those gaps — but any competitor who fills theirs in could take that spot.

Your Reviews Are Stale

You might have 200 reviews. That sounds great. But when was the last one?

Google does not just count reviews. It weighs how recent they are. One review from last week matters more to Google's algorithm than ten reviews from a year ago. I have seen businesses with 56 reviews outrank competitors with 800-plus — specifically because the smaller business had newer, more consistent reviews coming in.

What I look for in every audit is velocity. Are reviews coming in every week? Every few days? Or did they all come in during one push six months ago and then stop?

If you do not have a system for getting reviews consistently — not occasionally, not when you remember, but consistently — that is one of the first things to fix. Two to three new reviews per week is what I aim for with my clients. It does not have to be complicated. A simple text or email after every job with a direct link to your Google review page is enough to keep the engine running.

Your Website Is Not Telling Google What You Do

Your website might look great. Professional design, nice photos, clean layout. But Google does not see what you see. Google reads code.

What Google is looking for is very specific. Your title tag — the text that shows up in the browser tab — needs to include your main service and your city. Your H1 heading needs to match. Your address and phone number need to be hard-coded in the footer of every page, not buried in an image or hidden behind a "Contact Us" link.

When I audited Friendly Neighborhood Plumber here in Knoxville, their title tag and H1 were both perfect. "Plumbing Service in Knoxville, Tennessee." That is exactly what Google wants to see. Their website had service pages, area pages for every town they cover, and a clear call to action. That is the kind of website structure that tells Google: this business is real, it serves this area, and it does this specific thing.

Not every business I audit has this figured out. One Oak Ridge roofing company I reviewed recently did not have a website at all. Businesses from Knoxville — twenty minutes away — were ranking in Oak Ridge instead of them. Not because those businesses were better, but because they had websites with content and this company did not. Google cannot rank what does not exist.

Your Business Information Is Inconsistent

This one sounds small but it is not. Your business name, address, and phone number — what the industry calls NAP — needs to be identical everywhere. Not similar. Identical.

If your Google profile says "2503 W Beaver Creek Dr" and your website says "2503 West Beaver Creek Drive," that is a problem. If your Facebook page has a different phone number than your Google listing, that is a problem. If you moved two years ago and forgot to update your Yelp listing, that is still a problem right now.

Google cross-references your information across every platform it can find. When the details do not match, it loses confidence that your listing is legitimate. That doubt directly impacts your ranking. I find NAP inconsistencies in almost every audit I do — "Road" versus "Rd," "#5" versus "Suite 5," old phone numbers on forgotten directory listings. Each one is a crack in the foundation.

Fixing NAP consistency is one of the easiest and most impactful changes a local business can make. It costs nothing. It takes an afternoon. And it removes doubt from every algorithm Google runs on your business.

Your Website Is Slow on Mobile

Page speed is one of the few ranking factors Google has publicly confirmed affects where you show up. Not implied, not theorized — confirmed. And the test that matters is mobile, not desktop, because that is where the majority of local searches happen.

I look for a mobile PageSpeed score of at least 70. Most businesses I audit score in the 50s. The website looks fine when you visit it on your phone — it loads, everything works. But behind the scenes, the code is bloated. Oversized images, scripts loading before they need to, unused CSS files left over from the template. All of it adds fractions of a second, and Google notices.

When I audited a Knoxville tree service franchise, their desktop speed was fine but mobile was very slow. They were ranked twelfth — on the first page, but far from the top three. Mobile speed was one of the specific things holding them back.

You Have No Citations Beyond the Basics

Citations are listings of your business on directories and websites across the internet. Yelp, Facebook, Apple Maps, BBB, Nextdoor — those are the basics. Most established businesses have them by default or because someone set them up years ago.

But the basics are the floor, not the ceiling. Google's confidence in your business grows with every additional high-quality citation it finds. And there are hundreds of directories most business owners do not even know exist — niche-specific platforms, data aggregators, local chamber listings, even automotive company websites where you can create free business profiles.

One business I audited had a listing on the West Knox Chamber of Commerce. That single local citation carries more weight than a dozen generic national directories because it directly ties the business to the Knoxville community. The businesses ranking in the top three almost always have more citations, from more diverse sources, than the ones stuck on page two.

You Do Not Have Schema Markup

This is the most technical one, but it matters. Schema markup is a block of code on your website that packages your business information — name, address, phone, services, hours, service areas — in a format Google can read instantly without guessing.

Without schema, Google has to crawl your website and figure out on its own what your business is, where it is, and what it does. With schema, you are handing Google the answer key. It removes ambiguity and gives you a cleaner, more confident signal in Google's algorithm.

Most small businesses do not have it. Most do not even know it exists. But every business I help gets schema added as one of the first fixes because it is straightforward to implement and it immediately improves how Google understands the business.

What This Actually Looks Like in Practice

None of these problems require starting over. That is the part that surprises most business owners. They expect to hear that they need a brand new website, or that SEO is some mysterious black box that takes years to figure out.

It is not. It is a checklist. A specific, finite checklist.

Fill out your Google Business Profile completely. Get reviews consistently. Fix your NAP everywhere. Make your website tell Google what you do and where. Speed up your mobile site. Build citations. Add schema. Each of those is a concrete action with a concrete result.

The businesses that rank in the top three in Knoxville right now are not doing anything magical. They are just doing more of these things, more correctly, than the businesses below them. And most of the businesses below them have no idea these things even matter.
